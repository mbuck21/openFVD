# R13: force limits, second sources, and the check at every seat

Follow-up to R3 (`docs/research/r3-force-limits.md`). R3 listed twelve items that rest on one source or are uncertain. This note looks for a second public source for each, answers a few new questions about checking every seat of a train, and re-checks whether buying the standard is still worth it. All sources were read on 2026-11-13 and every link was accessed on that date. R3 itself is not changed. Where this note and R3 disagree, this note says so.

## Summary

1. **The 2006 edition of F2291 opens and is readable.** The archive.org copy (F2291-06a, from Public.Resource.Org) has a text layer. Its words are legible; the numbers printed *inside* the curve figures are mostly garbled in that text layer, so the break-point times of the curves could not be re-read from it. What it does give clearly is the rule text: clause numbers, the reversal rule, the weightless-to-positive rule, the restraint hardware classes, the definitions of base, over-the-shoulder and prone restraints, and the egg rule.
2. **Count for the twelve items of R3 (details in section 2):** 2 corroborated, 3 partly corroborated, 6 still single-source, 1 contradicted. With partly corroborated counted as single-source, that is 2 corroborated, 9 single-source, 1 contradicted. Most of the corroboration is from the 2006 edition only, so it says "this was already so in 2006", not "this is so in F2291-26b".
3. **One value is contradicted, and it matters for the check:** the egg radii. R3 says the radii are the 0.2 s limits (from Rohde's formulas). The 2006 text says the radii are the 0.2 s limits **times 1.1** (clause 7.1.5.1). Whether the 1.1 is still in the current edition is not known. A 10 % wider egg changes pass and fail near the edge.
4. **No public source gives the numbers of F2291-24, -25 or -26.** Searches found nothing on what changed. The ASTM store lists the editions only.
5. **For the per-seat check (section 3):** the standards measure at *one accelerometer position per car on the seat*, and the practice is to run the test in the front, middle and rear car. No public text says the limits "hold at every seat", and none says anything about inner against outer seats. The design reference is the heartline (Rohde). The head is treated as an extra, worked out from the rotation terms.
6. **Buying is still worth it (section 4):** F2291-26b is still active at US$121. A bought copy settles the Gz, -Gz and Gy times, the push-pull curve, the egg factor, the children's multiplier and what changed after 23b. None of that can be got from public sources.

> **Caution.** As in R3, this is a design aid for a hobby tool, not a certification. Secondary sources redraw or paraphrase copyrighted standards, an OCR layer can misread digits, and the 2006 edition is old. A "corroborated" below means two public sources agree. It does not mean the number has been checked against the current standard.

### What the 2006 edition (N6) is, and is not

- It is F2291-06a, with its own numbering: acceleration limits are **Section 7**, the restraint diagram is Fig. 2 with the rules in 6.4.3, the references to measurement are to F2137 Section 12. The 2023 edition's preview (IT) also has acceleration limits as Section 7 and the restraint diagram in 6.4.3.
- R3 says that in 26b acceleration limits are section 6 and restraints section 5 (from the store abstract). The 23b store page now gives "Section 6" too, while the 23b preview (IT) says 7. These two public statements disagree and cannot be settled without the standard. **Do not hard-code clause numbers into the limit file**; record the number with the edition it came from.
- The 2006 edition has no children's multiplier. Clause 7.1.4.3 says its limits are for patrons 48 in. and above and leaves smaller riders to the designer, with a review by a biodynamic expert if the limits are exceeded.

### Smaller findings that are not in R3's list

- **R3's contradiction about +Gx at 12 s is settled in favour of 2.5 g.** N6 Fig. 6 has "2.5" and "12" and the headrest note (headrest needed above 1.5 g unless onset is below 5 g/s, in which case 2.0 g is allowed; without a headrest at most 4 s above 1.5 g). Rohde's "12 s (max. 2 g, not shown)" in the worked example looks like a slip. Medium to high.
- **The weight of the limits at 90 s:** N6 7.1.4.6 and R agree that nothing beyond 90 s is addressed.
- **Impact:** N6 7.1.4.2: impacts shorter than 200 ms are not addressed. This is the same in the 2023 preview (IT, definitions 3.1.1 and 3.1.2).
- **Units and gravity:** N6 7.1.4.5 says the limits are "total net acceleration, inclusive of earth's gravity": 1 g in the vertical axis at rest. Confirms R3's section 1.
- **EN 13814-1 Annex I structure** (EN, headings only): I.1 medical tolerance; I.2.3 X, I.2.4 Y, I.2.5 Z (parallel to the spine), I.2.6 combinations; I.3.1 reversals in X and Y; I.3.2 transitions in Z; I.4 an example for obtaining admissible accelerations. The annex is informative. This matches the rules R3 lists (reversal, transitions, combinations), but no EN value was seen.
- **A different document, ISO/TS 17929:2014** ("Biomechanical effects on amusement ride passengers"), is the source for PE's numbers: 6 g not longer than 1 s, 5 g for 2 s, 15 g/s onset, and a trailing-edge rate of 0.8 g/s as a trapezium envelope. The 2020 CPSC report says ISO wanted to fold this TS into ISO 17842, and the F24 group would check it. It is not F2291; do not mix its numbers with the limit file. It shows the limits in standards have differed.
- **Things not found at all:** the first edition of Rohde's paper (the second edition's preface says the first presentation was in Louisville, Kentucky in February 2024; no copy found); Rohde's 2018 *Steel Construction* paper (<https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/stco.201810026>) is about loads and strengths, abstract only; IAAPA, AIMS or NAARSO slide decks (none found); William Bussone's talks on F2291, F2137 and F1159 (his CV lists a talk on 26 February 2020 and "Biomechanics of amusement rides and devices" on 5 March 2025, in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania; the slides are not linked, <https://www.cbeconsultants.com/wp-content/uploads/WRBussone_CV.pdf>); a TUV publication with the curves; any text of F2291-24, -25 or -26.

What this means for the program, in plain words: a real ride is measured at one fixed point per car, a little above the seat, in a few cars chosen by the tester. Nothing public says that every seat has been or must be checked. A per-seat check in FVD++ goes beyond what the test practice does. That is fine as a design aid, but a failure at an outer seat on a wide train, or a pass at the heartline with a head that sees more, is not something the standard itself tells you to look for. It should be reported as extra, with the offset used.

### Reference point and offsets

- **Design is at the heartline.** Rohde 7.6.2: "it is generally assumed that the design calculation is based on the heart line". The EN test puts the accelerometer about there; the ASTM test sits lower (about 30 to 41 cm for adults against about 56 to 66 cm for EN). Rohde calls the difference "normally ... not significant", but says in loops the head can see more than is measured.
- **The head, with a distance D from the heartline:** a head acceleration of ω² · R from the rotation rate, and α · R from the change of rotation rate, for roll about the longitudinal axis and pitch about the lateral axis. Yaw gives "negligible" head forces. With "upper torso restraints" only the head moves relative to the seat. A positive roll rate gives "eyes up" at the head. R 7.6.2, Figs. 7.29 to 7.31. Rohde gives no value for D. R3's formula (section 7 of R3) is the same physics for any seat.
- **No public number for seat pan to heart or to head.** What exists: the accelerometer height ranges above, and EN 13814's note that seat backs be at least 0.4 m high (SK, secondary, not a design offset). R3's proposal stands: make the offsets fields of the train type (seat height of the heart, and of the head if wanted), and take the starting values from the F2137 ranges (heart about 22 to 26 in. above the seat for adults).
- **Rotations are not limited by the standards and not measured in the SARC test,** so the accelerometer, which is "relatively far away from the head", does not see head effects (R 7.6.2 summary).

### How per-seat or per-car results are reported in public

- **The Restraint Rose** (Rohde, Fig. 5.3, from Vekoma): the measured sustained x-z accelerations of one test, filtered at 1 Hz and plotted on the restraint determination diagram, one per test (so per car position and per load). This is the one public example of a per-car figure.
- **Time-history charts** for each axis, with the slicing method, per test (R Fig. 5.2, 7.8, 7.8a). No public report with limits drawn against seat numbers was found.
- **Front against rear:** Rohde 10: in loops "the first cars have a much higher speed than the last ones", so the front and rear cars see different accelerations; superelevation is built for one design speed, so individual cars can see lateral accelerations that "can exceed the limits in borderline cases". This agrees with R3 section 7 and with the front-against-rear question that R7 looks at.
- **Teaching papers** (not found opened): Alberghi, Pezzi and Ortolani, *Is it more thrilling to ride at the front or the back of a roller coaster?*, The Physics Teacher 45 (2007) 536, which measures vertical acceleration by seat position (cited in PE; ResearchGate page seen only in a search result). A Busch Gardens physics-day workbook asks students to compare front and back. Neither is a ride analysis.
- **The one online calculator found (UC)** works per element, not per seat. It does not model front against rear.

| EN 13814: a "restraint rose" need not be applied if braking is at most 0.7 g, the downward slope is at most 40 degrees and the head-to-foot acceleration is above +0.2 g everywhere. | search-result summary only | low |

For the train type, this supports R3's two separate fields. The second field, `restraint_kind`, can take its values straight from 7.1.4.7: base case, over-the-shoulder, prone. A lap-bar train with no shoulder restraint would normally be the base case with the Class 4 or 5 hardware. That is an inference, not a public statement.

### The three most useful findings of Part 2

1. The test practice measures at one point per car, 12 to 16 in. above the seat (ASTM) or 22 to 26 in. (EN), in the front, middle and rear car. It does not check every seat. A per-seat check is an extension and should be labelled so.
2. The design reference is the heartline, the head is a separate calculation from rotation rate and rotational acceleration at a distance D, and the standards do not limit rotation. No public number gives D.
3. In F2291-06a 7.1.4.7 the three -Gx lines are tied to named restraint types: base case (Class 4 or 5), over-the-shoulder (Class 5) and prone. The train type's `restraint_kind` can use those names.
